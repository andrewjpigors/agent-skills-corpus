# Aussie Culture (Melbourne / Victoria lean)

Knowledge base for writing and reasoning about Australian culture — weighted toward Melbourne and Victoria. The goal is content that a local reads without flinching: right references, right register, no tourist clichés, and an instinct for what quietly grates.

This is the **substance** layer. Its companions handle the rest:

- `aus-business-english` — spelling (EN-AU) and tone for professional comms. *How* to write.
- `aus-electrical-handbook` — trade terminology, standards, licensing. *What's true* about the work.
- This skill — the cultural references, values, and judgement that make copy feel local. *What to draw on.*

They co-activate constantly. When you're writing a suburb page or a blog post, you'll usually want all three.

## When to use this skill

- Writing or editing public copy: blog posts, suburb landing pages, service descriptions, project case studies, About / careers pages.
- Drafting customer-facing comms — emails, SMS, quote intros, callback scripts — that should sound like a local sparky's outfit, not a call centre.
- Reviewing copy (yours or a supplier's) for anything that reads as foreign, forced, or culturally tone-deaf.
- Answering questions about Australian / Melbourne customs, sport, food, weather, slang, or holidays.
- Anything touching First Nations, Anzac Day, or Australia Day, where getting the register wrong does real damage.

If you're producing prose for a local audience, default to consulting this — the failure mode is *under*-triggering and shipping copy that's subtly off.

## The cultural operating system

Most "make it sound Australian" requests are really about register, not vocabulary. You don't sprinkle slang on; you write from a different set of defaults. These are the values that shape how Australians read tone — get these right and the copy lands even with zero slang in it.

**The fair go / egalitarianism.** Deep instinct that nobody's better than anyone else. Hierarchy is tolerated, deference isn't. The tradesperson and the customer talk as equals. In copy: no talking down, no talking up, no "we're honoured to serve you." Just straight across.

**The BS detector.** Australians are quick to smell — and allergic to — hype, spin, and hard-sell. Superlatives ("the best", "premium", "world-class", "unrivalled") read as red flags, not selling points. Credibility is *earned by evidence and demonstrated by understatement*: show the work, name the specifics, let the reader conclude you're good. Don't claim it.

**Tall poppy syndrome.** The flip side — open self-promotion gets cut down. "Award-winning" and "industry-leading" make a local wary, not impressed. Better to state plainly what you do and how long you've done it. Confidence is fine; skiting (bragging) isn't.

**Understatement and self-deprecation.** "Not bad" is high praise. "We had a bit of a shocker" can mean a disaster. A business that can gently take the mickey out of itself reads as trustworthy; one that takes itself too seriously reads as up itself. Dry, deadpan humour is the native register — never zany or exclamation-marked.

**Mateship and "having a go."** Loyalty, pitching in, backing people who turn up and put in the effort. Effort and reliability are respected more than polish. "We turn up when we say we will" beats "premium customer experience" every time — it's the actual thing locals complain about with tradies.

**She'll be right / no fuss.** A calm, unflustered, can-do attitude. Problems get sorted, not catastrophised. But read the room: "no worries" is right for "happy to help", wrong for "your switchboard's been arcing for three days." (Same note as in `aus-business-english`.)

**How this shapes writing, concretely:**

- Lead with what you actually do, in plain words. Cut the superlatives.
- Prefer specifics over adjectives: "20 years on the tools in Melbourne's north" > "highly experienced team."
- Warm and direct beats formal and effusive. A bit of dry humour is welcome; forced ocker is not.
- Let proof do the persuading — reviews, real jobs, how long you've been around, turning up on time.
- Never grovel, never boast. Talk to the reader like a capable equal.

## Authenticity guardrails — the cringe list

These are the tells that copy was written by someone whose idea of Australia comes from tourism ads. Avoid all of them unless knowingly used for irony:

- **"Throw a shrimp on the barbie"** — it's a *prawn*, not a shrimp. The phrase is a 1980s tourism-ad import no Australian says.
- **"Crikey", "strewth", "fair dinkum", "struth", "by jingo"** — Steve Irwin / Crocodile Dundee cosplay. Dead in real speech.
- **"G'day mate" every other line, "mate" as punctuation** — once, naturally placed, is fine. Stacked up, it's a costume.
- **Drop bears, dangerous-animals-everywhere, "watch out for snakes/spiders"** — drop bears are a joke played on tourists; the deadly-wildlife schtick is an outsider's framing.
- **Everyone lives near the outback / a beach / has a kangaroo out the back** — Australia is one of the most urbanised countries on earth. Your reader lives in a Melbourne suburb, not the bush.
- **Fosters** — a beer brewed largely for export. Nobody here drinks it. Don't reference it as "the Australian beer."
- **"Aussie Aussie Aussie, Oi Oi Oi", boxing kangaroos, Vegemite eaten by the spoonful** — flag-waving caricature.
- **Calling the country "Straya" in earnest, "Aussie battler" clichés** — fine ironically among locals, cringe in marketing copy written *at* them.

**The reality to write toward instead:** suburban, coastal, highly multicultural, café-and-footy, deeply unimpressed by anyone trying too hard. Melbourne especially is a big, diverse, design-literate city — write to that, not to a postcard.

## Melbourne / Victoria essentials

The local knowledge that signals you're actually from here:

- **Footy = AFL.** Full stop. In Melbourne, "the footy" is Australian Rules, never rugby or soccer. Ten of the eighteen AFL clubs are Victorian; tribal allegiance runs deep. Soccer is "soccer" (or "the A-League"); rugby is a NSW/Queensland thing.
- **The 'G.** The Melbourne Cricket Ground — footy in winter, the Boxing Day Test in summer. A genuine civic landmark.
- **Two local public holidays outsiders forget:** the **AFL Grand Final Friday** (the eve of the Grand Final, late Sept/early Oct — Victoria only) and **Melbourne Cup Day** (first Tuesday in November, "the race that stops a nation", a public holiday across metro Melbourne). Also **Labour Day**, second Monday in March.
- **North vs south of the river (the Yarra).** A running half-joke about identity — northsiders supposedly never cross to the south and vice versa. Good for warmth; don't overplay.
- **Trams, Myki, the hook turn.** Melbourne runs the world's largest tram network; you tap on with a *Myki* card; in the CBD you do the famous *hook turn* (turn right from the left lane, to clear the trams). Pure local shorthand.
- **Coffee.** Melbourne takes its coffee seriously and quietly believes it's the best in the world. The flat white, the local roaster, the laneway café — all genuine sources of pride.
- **"Four seasons in one day."** The standard, accurate complaint about Melbourne weather. Summers can hit the low 40s; winters are cool and grey rather than freezing. Practical for trades copy (heatwave load, storm season).
- **Potato cake**, not potato scallop. A **parma** (chicken parmigiana) at the pub. **Pot** and **pint** for beer — "schooner" is the northern states.
- **Multicultural by default.** Greek, Italian, Vietnamese, Chinese, Indian, Lebanese and many more communities are woven through the suburbs. The all-white "Aussie battler" image is both inaccurate and dated — write the real, mixed city.

## Cultural sensitivities — get these right

A few areas where the wrong register isn't just unfunny, it's offensive. When in doubt, read the relevant reference file before writing.

- **First Nations.** Use "Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples" or "First Nations / First Peoples". Never "Aborigine" as a noun (dated, offensive). An **Acknowledgement of Country** can be offered by anyone; a **Welcome to Country** is given only by Traditional Owners. Melbourne sits on the lands of the Wurundjeri and Boon Wurrung peoples of the Kulin Nation. Don't tokenise. See `references/cultural-sensitivity.md`.
- **Anzac Day (25 April).** Sacred and solemn — dawn services, "Lest We Forget", the RSL, two-up. Never flippant, never a sales hook ("Anzac Day blowout sale" is genuinely offensive, and using the word "Anzac" commercially is legally restricted). Anzac biscuits are fine; trivialising the day is not.
- **Australia Day (26 January).** Genuinely contested — celebrated by many, mourned by others as Invasion Day / Survival Day. Treat with care; don't assume universal celebration, don't preach either way. Neutral and aware beats both jingoism and lecturing.
- **New Zealand.** The rivalry is affectionate banter, not real animosity (pavlova, Phar Lap, Crowded House, Russell Crowe, Split Enz — the eternal "who's claiming whom" game). Keep it light.
- **Don't conflate Australia with the US or UK.** No Thanksgiving, no "college", no "Main Street", no Bonfire Night. Australian institutions, seasons (Christmas is *summer*), and references only.

## Trade-culture note

When writing for an electrical contractor or trades business, the cultural and commercial reality overlap:

- The thing locals actually complain about with tradies is **not turning up, not calling back, and dodgy work.** "Reliable, licensed, turns up when we say we will" is a stronger promise than any superlative — it answers the real anxiety.
- Tradie culture has its own warmth: the ute, smoko (the morning tea break), knock-off (end of day), word-of-mouth ("know a good sparky?"). You can nod to it lightly without putting on an act.
- Be careful around **"cash jobs"** — culturally familiar, but never something to endorse or wink at in copy for a licensed, compliant business.
- "Sparky" (electrician), "chippie" (carpenter), "plumber" — the trades have an easy banter among themselves. Friendly, never to the customer's expense.
